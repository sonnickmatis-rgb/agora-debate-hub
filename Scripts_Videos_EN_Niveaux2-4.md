## Level 2 · Module 1 · Provider or deployer
**File:** `niveau2-module1-en.mp4` · **Avatar:** same as the French version (woman)

Welcome to Level two. One question will guide us: who is responsible?

The AI Act distinguishes two main roles. The deployer is the one who uses an AI system in its business. When your teams work with Copilot or with an HR tool, your company is a deployer. The provider, on the other hand, develops a system and places it on the market under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge.

The same company can wear both hats. A digital services company is a deployer when it uses AI tools internally… and it becomes a provider as soon as it delivers a chatbot or software to a client under its own brand. The role is therefore determined system by system, use by use.

And there is a trap: you can become a provider without meaning to. For example, by reconfiguring a general-purpose assistant so that it scores candidates in a recruitment process. By changing the purpose of the tool, you create a high-risk system… and you become responsible for it, with heavy obligations.

In this module, you will follow Karim, in pre-sales, and Tom, a developer. With them, you will learn to identify your role before delivering anything, and to spot the moment when a simple setting changes everything.

One reflex to keep in mind: as soon as we deliver under our own name, we are the provider.

## Level 2 · Module 2 · Models and transparency
**File:** `niveau2-module2-en.mp4` · **Avatar:** woman

In this module, we talk about large AI models, and about transparency.

Large models, such as GPT, Claude or Mistral, are called general-purpose AI models. Good news: using them, or even fine-tuning them on your own documents, generally does not make you the provider of the model. But the tool you build with them remains your responsibility.

This is where transparency comes in. Since the second of August, twenty twenty-six, Article fifty has imposed several obligations. When a person interacts with a chatbot, they must know they are talking to an AI, from the very first exchange. Content generated by AI, such as images, sounds or videos, must be identifiable. And deepfakes, content that imitates a real person, must be disclosed.

Imagine a chatbot that introduces itself as "Julie, adviser", with a photo, without ever saying it is an AI. Friendly? Perhaps. Compliant? No.

Conversely, using Copilot to write an email or code internally does not trigger any of these obligations. Transparency protects the people exposed to the system, not your internal use.

In this module, you will learn to tell the difference, and to know when and how to inform people.

## Level 3 · Module 1 · Prohibited practices
**File:** `niveau3-module1-en.mp4` · **Avatar:** man

Welcome to Level three: the red lines.

Some uses of AI are simply prohibited in Europe. Since the second of February, twenty twenty-five, Article five has listed eight banned practices. Among them: manipulating behaviour in a deceptive way, exploiting the vulnerability of certain people, or giving individuals a social score.

For a company, one prohibition is particularly concrete: emotion recognition in the workplace. A tool that analyses employees' voices or faces to detect their stress, anger or engagement is prohibited, even if it is presented as a well-being tool. Only medical or safety reasons are exceptions. And this prohibition also applies to job candidates.

But watch out for the nuances: what is not prohibited is not necessarily free. Analysing customers' emotions, for example, remains possible, but strictly regulated.

And the list is growing. From the second of December, twenty twenty-six, two new prohibitions are added: realistic intimate images or videos of a person, created without their consent, and child sexual abuse material.

In this module, you will follow Karim as he faces a tricky client request. You will learn to recognise a red line, and to dare to say no when necessary.

## Level 3 · Module 2 · High-risk HR
**File:** `niveau3-module2-en.mp4` · **Avatar:** man

In this module, we look at high-risk uses, and in particular at human resources.

The AI Act does not ban AI in HR, but it regulates it strictly. Sorting applications, assessing candidates, deciding on a promotion or monitoring employees' performance: these uses are classified as high risk. Why? Because a mistake by the tool can have serious consequences for a person's working life.

The obligations linked to these uses will apply from the second of December, twenty twenty-seven. In particular, they will require human oversight by trained people, and informing employees and their representatives before the tool is put into service.

Beware of a common misconception: it is not enough for a human to sign the final decision. If the tool did the sorting and shaped the judgement, it is still high risk.

In Luxembourg, labour law goes even further, and already today. Before introducing a monitoring tool, the company must inform its staff delegation. And from one hundred and fifty employees, a tool that monitors performance requires the delegation's agreement.

In this module, you will follow Sofia, HR director, who has to sort eight hundred applications. With her, you will learn to recognise a high-risk use, and to follow the right steps before any deployment.

## Level 4 · Module 1 · Data and GDPR
**File:** `niveau4-module1-en.mp4` · **Avatar:** woman

Welcome to the final level: our everyday reflexes.

Let's start with the most common risk. It's not a spectacular fine, it's a simple copy and paste. Pasting names, salaries or bank details into a consumer AI tool means exposing information that should never leave the company.

Because the AI Act does not replace the GDPR: it adds to it. As soon as personal data goes into an AI tool, the GDPR applies, and it applies today. In Luxembourg, almost half of the data breaches reported to the CNPD in twenty twenty-five were caused by human error.

Before using AI, ask yourself three questions. Is it a prohibited or high-risk use? What data am I going to put into the tool? And who needs to be informed?

The people concerned also have rights. If a decision about them was made with the help of AI, they must be able to obtain an understandable explanation. And no, sending the algorithm's source code is not enough.

In this module, you will follow Nora, a consultant, at the end of a Friday afternoon. With her, you will adopt the right reflexes: a tool approved by the company, protected data, and doubts always reported.

## Level 4 · Module 2 · Incidents, penalties, authorities
**File:** `niveau4-module2-en.mp4` · **Avatar:** woman

To finish this course, let's see what happens when something goes wrong.

Imagine a chatbot that, by mistake, displays one client's data to another. Several texts then apply at the same time. The GDPR requires the data breach to be reported within seventy-two hours. And for clients in the financial sector, other rules, such as DORA, also come into play. The right reflex: never fix it quietly, but raise the alarm immediately.

On the AI Act side, penalties can be heavy: up to thirty-five million euros or seven per cent of worldwide turnover for a prohibited practice, and up to fifteen million or three per cent for a breach of the transparency rules. These are ceilings, and it is the company that pays, not the employee. SMEs benefit from lower ceilings, but they are not exempt.

And in Luxembourg, who will supervise? The regulation already applies directly. A draft law plans to entrust this role mainly to the CNPD, but in October twenty twenty-six, it had not yet been passed. The CNPD is nevertheless already answering your questions about AI.

In this module, you will learn to respond to an incident, to understand the penalty regime, and to know who to turn to. This is the last step before completing your course. Enjoy the end of your training!
